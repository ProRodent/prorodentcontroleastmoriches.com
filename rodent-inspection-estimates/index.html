Why start with a look and not a trap.

A trap solves a trapping problem. If the real issue is a gap at the roofline, a loose dryer vent or a smell from a pipe chase, then the trap is money spent on the wrong end of the house. An inspection costs a visit, and it can spare you several wasted purchases.

It also protects against an easy mistake: treating one visible mouse as the whole problem. Rodents seldom travel alone, and the animal you saw in the kitchen may be the least of several using the same hole.

What the technician checks, in order.

  • Exterior: foundation edges, vents, pipe and wire entries, door seals, roof edges and any burrows.
  • Garage and storage: lower shelves, corners, stored boxes and the seam where slab meets wall.
  • Living areas: below sinks, behind appliances, within the pantry and by the baseboards.
  • Attic or crawl space: insulation condition, droppings, nesting piles, wire and duct damage.

Between steps the technician photographs findings and marks them on a simple sketch of the building. That sketch is what turns a vague noise into a list of numbered openings.

The coastal checklist.

Buildings near the water get a few extra questions. Are vent screens rusted? Do crawl space doors close tightly after storms? Is there a pile of driftwood, plywood or boat gear against a wall? Salt air may weaken cheap hardware, and storm wrack can hide gaps, so those items go on the list for every South Shore property.

Getting ready for the visit.

Open up access to the roof hatch and underfloor door, and shut the pets in another room. Write down the dates and hours of any sounds, because patterns help. If you have already set traps or laid bait, say exactly where, since a stray device can confuse the picture.

For urgent cases, the rat and mouse pages describe what an emergency visit looks like, and a live voice answers whenever you call.

Before the crew arrives.

  • If leaves or twigs fill the engine bay, keep the car parked pending a check.
  • Move lawn seed, dog chow and feeder mix inside for now.
  • Bag up one chewed item as evidence for whoever visits.
  • Find the breaker panel in case a chewed cable has to be shut off.

Before you call: five habits that keep dust down.

Public health sources agree on most of this, so the common ground is condensed into the steps below.

  • If the tool in your hand is dry, put it down: brooms, vacuums, compressed air and dusters all qualify.
  • Create a cross-breeze, wait roughly half an hour, and only then step in without sniffing around.
  • Skin should not meet pellets, urine spots or shredded nest material, so wear gloves from the first minute.
  • Treat the surface beneath and around the waste as well, because urine spreads further than the stain shows.
  • Ordinary garbage service takes the bag, and any reusable tool you used gets rinsed.
  • Illness after contact with rodent waste is a physician's question, so tell the doctor exactly what you touched.

Questions about inspections.

How much time should I set aside for the visit?

Size and layout decide it. A modest house with a simple hatch goes fast, while a big lot with a crawl space and outbuildings runs longer. Describe the layout and the dispatcher offers a rough duration.

Do I have to be home?

Someone should be present for the walk-through, or you should arrange access to every area in question. Locked sheds and attics are the usual snags.

What if you find nothing?

Then you hear exactly that, along with the likeliest sources of the sound or smell. Squirrels, loose insulation, ductwork and plumbing can all mimic rodents.

Does the estimate commit me to anything?

No. You approve line by line, and a step you decline simply drops off the list.

Can I get an inspection for a business?

Yes, and the commercial page describes how delivery doors, storerooms and shared walls are covered.
