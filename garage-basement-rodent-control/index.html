A garage is a short drive from shelter and food.

Garages combine the three things rodents want. Shelter comes from cardboard, old furniture and boxes of seasonal gear. Food may be a bag of grass seed, pet kibble or a bird feeder stored over winter. Access comes from a door seal that has hardened and pulled away at the corners.

Because garages are rarely heated, they feel like a safe in-between: out of the weather but not inside the house. Animals use them as a staging area, and once they are comfortable there, the shared wall to the kitchen is only a few inches away.

What the technician looks at first.

  • The bottom edge of the garage door, including both corners and the seal's condition.
  • The seam where the slab meets the wall, which is a common crack line.
  • Pipe, wire and vent penetrations through the walls.
  • Stored items, especially cardboard, paper, fabric and anything that has not moved in months.

A flashlight laid flat along the floor shows light gaps that a standing eye misses. We often find that the entrance is somewhere unglamorous, like a bent corner of the door or a missing weep hole cover.

Basements, drains and damp corners.

Some South Shore houses have full basements, others have a crawl space or a slab, and plenty have a sump pit or floor drain. Each of those is a possible route. We check drain screens and sump lids, look for gaps where pipes enter through the foundation wall, and note any damp areas that might widen cracks.

If the building has a finished basement, check the ceiling and the soffits where ducts run. Mice follow ducts and wiring up into the living space.

Closing the shared wall.

The wall between garage and house deserves special care. Gaps around pipes, wiring, the dryer vent and the attached door can let animals pass from one to the other. Closing them with metal and sealant keeps a garage problem from becoming a kitchen problem.

See our exclusion page for materials, and the mouse page if activity has already reached the pantry.

A spring and fall routine.

Do a quick check twice a year: once before cold weather arrives and once after the thaw. Look for new pellets, chewed corners, nesting scraps and any daylight under the door. Fix small problems then, and they stay small.

While you wait.

  • Make the garage wall, the cabinet below the basin and the ceiling hatch easy to reach.
  • Do not shake out chewed cardboard, because that lifts fine dust.
  • Keep any snap traps you bought far from toddlers and large dogs.
  • Identify the room with the strongest odor, which becomes the first stop.

Before you call: a safer way to tidy droppings.

Come back to this list whenever pellets, urine stains or torn bedding appear indoors.

  • Dry crumbly debris stays where it lies until dampened, as CDC advice discourages brushing or vacuuming it.
  • Create a cross-breeze, wait roughly half an hour, and only then step in without sniffing around.
  • Skin should not meet pellets, urine spots or shredded nest material, so wear gloves from the first minute.
  • Treat the surface beneath and around the waste as well, because urine spreads further than the stain shows.
  • Add a second bag when the first is thin or heavy.
  • Our expertise ends at pest work, so a clinician should handle any sickness that follows.

Lower-level and outbuilding queries.

What makes a garage so inviting to them?

It offers shelter, stored goods to chew and often a poorly sealed door. Add pet food or birdseed in a bin and the building becomes a dining room too.

Does a damp basement make it worse?

Moisture attracts some pests and softens building materials, which can widen gaps. Rodents care less about damp than about food, warmth and cover, but a wet corner is a likely entry.

Should I throw away cardboard boxes?

Cardboard is easy to chew and easy to nest in, so plastic bins with snap lids are better for anything you keep. Bag chewed boxes and discard them following the CDC steps.

What if I have a sump pit?

Mention it. Sump pits and floor drains can be routes, and the technician checks lids and screens.

Is the garage door seal really that important?

It often is. A worn seal leaves a gap along the bottom, and mice only need a pencil-width opening.

Garage facts worth sharing.

Does daylight show under the door? What leans against the walls? Which box has sat untouched since last year? A basement, utility room or shed may need the same attention.
