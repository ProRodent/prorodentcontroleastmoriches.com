Reopening a Shut House in Spring: What to Check Before You Unpack

The first hour in a house that has been shut all winter is the best time to find a rodent problem. Check before you unpack, because your bags make wonderful nest material.

Arriving at a house you have not seen since October is a pleasant moment. You open the door, stretch, carry in the bags, and start planning the season. This article asks you to slow down for ten minutes first.

A shut house is quiet, and rodents love quiet. If anything moved in over the winter, the evidence is easiest to see right now, before foot traffic and cleaning scatter it.

The first ten minutes.

Before you carry in luggage, walk through each room with the lights on. Look at floors, corners, counters and shelves. Open cabinets under sinks and the pantry. Listen for scratching or movement in walls and ceilings. Sniff for a musty, sharp or sweet smell.

  • Open the attic hatch and look at the insulation and any stored items.
  • Check the kitchen cabinets, drawers and pantry shelves.
  • Look behind and under appliances.
  • Inspect the garage corners, the water heater closet and any storage rooms.
  • Lift the covers on boats, furniture and equipment before moving them.

If you find signs.

A few pellets in a corner can come from a single visitor months ago. Many pellets, shredded fabric or paper, or a nest in a drawer suggest a longer stay. Whichever it is, do not reach for the broom. Dried pellets disintegrate, and inhaling that dust is exactly what the CDC cautions against.

Systems before comforts.

Resist the urge to flip every switch. Rodents sometimes nest in air handlers, dryer vents and around water heaters. Check filters, vent covers and exhaust ducts before running anything. If you see chewed insulation or nesting material, leave the system off until it is cleared.

Look at the electrical panel, too. Wires with exposed copper are a fire risk. If you see chewing, stay away and call an electrician.

Airing and wet cleaning.

Let outside air flow through for thirty minutes or so before cleaning, and keep out of the room during that time. Put on gloves, spray droppings with disinfectant until wet, wait several minutes and wipe with paper towels. Bag the waste. Details are in the CDC box below.

Unpacking without feeding anything.

Once you have checked and cleaned, unpack with a plan. Store food in lidded containers from day one. Keep bags and boxes off the floor, and break down cardboard rather than stacking it. Pet food belongs in a sealed tub.

When to call.

  • You find a nest, or many fresh droppings in more than one room.
  • You hear activity in walls or ceilings.
  • You see chewed wiring, insulation or hoses.
  • A smell does not fade after airing.

A technician can inspect, trap and seal, and the inspection page describes the first visit. Prompt action in May is far less painful than discovering a colony in July.

Before you call, hold the broom.

You can follow it without ever calling us; agencies hand the very same advice to every household that finds droppings indoors.

  • Leave the broom and the shop vacuum in the closet, because dry droppings crumble and the dust goes straight into your lungs.
  • Open every opening in the room, then do something else for thirty minutes before you return.
  • Gloves go on at the start and soap and water come at the end.
  • Treat the surface beneath and around the waste as well, because urine spreads further than the stain shows.
  • Drop soiled towels and any dead animal into one plastic bag, using gloves or tongs, then tie it and place it in the household trash.
  • Keep tabs on your health across the next few weeks, and see a physician should you develop flu-like illness or labored breathing.

What people ask next.

What if I find droppings on arrival?

Stop and avoid sweeping or vacuuming. Use the CDC box below, air the room for thirty minutes, and phone us when you see more than a scattering of pellets.

How do I tell old droppings from new?

Fresh droppings are dark and moist-looking. Old ones are gray, dry and crumbly. Either way, use the wet method for cleanup.

Should I open all the windows?

Opening doors and windows to air the house is a good idea, especially before cleanup. Close screens, and keep an eye out for animals that may bolt.

Can I turn on the heat and AC immediately?

Check vents, filters and ducts first. Rodents sometimes nest in ductwork or around the furnace, and running a system can spread dust and odors.

Is a few droppings a big deal?

It depends. A few pellets may be an old visitor, while many in several rooms suggest a settled colony. Call for an inspection if you are unsure.
