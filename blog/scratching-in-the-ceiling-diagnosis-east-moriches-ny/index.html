Scratching in the Ceiling: Working Out What It Is

Noise overhead is easy to hear and hard to interpret. Hour, sound type and the route it follows reveal plenty before anybody ventures up the attic ladder.

You are in bed, the house is quiet, and something starts to scratch above the ceiling. It is an unsettling sound, and it is easy to jump to the worst conclusion. A calmer approach is to treat it as a puzzle: when, where and what does it sound like?

Timing tells a story.

Mice and rats are mostly active at night, so scratching that begins after dark and quiets by morning fits them well. Squirrels tend to be active in daytime, often early morning, with heavier sounds like running and thudding. Birds nesting in a vent flutter and chirp at dawn. Bats, which we do not handle, make soft scrabbling and chirping around dusk.

Where the sound travels.

Follow the sound. Note whether it stays in one place, such as over a bedroom, or moves along a wall. Rodents often follow set routes along framing and wires. If the sound moves from the ceiling into a wall and then to the kitchen, that is a travel route worth mentioning.

Check the attic hatch with a flashlight. Watch for pellets, chewed cable, ripped batting and tracks wandering across the insulation. Do not touch anything. If you see signs, close the hatch and note what you saw.

Signs that confirm rodents.

  • Dark, rice-size pellets (mice) or larger capsule-shaped ones (rats).
  • Greasy rub marks along beams and openings.
  • Shredded insulation or paper forming a nest.
  • Gnawed wiring or wood.
  • A stale, musky smell that grows stronger near the attic.

What to do tonight.

Stay calm and don't poke the ceiling. Keep food put away and close the attic hatch. Do not run bait stations in the attic. Poison can kill an animal inside a wall, which leads to a smell that lasts for weeks.

Make notes: when it starts, how long it lasts, what it sounds like and where it is. These details help us choose the right plan. Our mouse control page and attic page explain what follows.

Why the noise often starts in autumn.

Cooler nights push animals toward warm, protected spaces. A house with a small gap near the roofline is a convenient choice. If you hear activity for the first time in October, check the eaves, vents and roof edges for gaps. On the South Shore, salt air can corrode screens and open those gaps, so inspect them yearly.

When the answer is not a rodent.

If the sounds fit squirrels, birds or bats, a rodent technician may not be the right person, and we will say so. Wildlife rules differ for bats and some birds, and a licensed wildlife professional handles them. We would rather point you in the right direction than sell a service that does not fit.

Before you close the tab.

What if the noise is only at dawn and dusk?

Timing at the edges of day can point to squirrels or birds, which are active in daylight, or to bats. Say so on the phone, since the answer shapes the plan.

Could it be pipes or ducts?

Yes. Metal that expands as it warms or cools can tick, and water in pipes can gurgle. Those sounds are rhythmic and tied to heating cycles or taps, unlike the irregular patter of an animal.

Should I go into the attic myself?

A flashlight from the hatch opening is enough for a first look. Avoid stepping between joists and do not disturb insulation, which may hide droppings and wires.

Does it matter if the noise stopped?

A gap in activity does not mean the problem left. Rodents often move between nests and food, and quiet can be temporary.

What do you need to know when I call?

Tell us the room, the time of night, whether the noise is scratching, rolling or thumping, and anything you saw or smelled. It helps the first visit move faster.
