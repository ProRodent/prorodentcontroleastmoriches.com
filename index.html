How rodents find a South Shore house.

East Moriches is a hamlet in the Town of Brookhaven, on Long Island's South Shore in Suffolk County. Wikipedia lists the hamlet's motto as "Long Island's Best Kept Secret." Whatever the secret is, mice and rats seem to have heard it, and what draws them is rarely the street name. A warm building with a gap and a snack nearby is all they need.

Shoreline weather adds its own wrinkles. Salt in the air may corrode cheap steel vent screens, sandy ground drains quickly and can settle under steps and slabs, and a nor'easter can leave wrack and branches stacked against siding. Treat these as general conditions along the coast and not a description of your property. They do hint at the first places worth a look: low vents, foundation corners, door sweeps and anything resting on the ground.

Which animal is it?

Droppings answer most of the question. Mouse pellets look like dark grains of rice, and a mouse can slip through a crack about as wide as a pencil. Rat droppings are fatter and blunter, and a rat needs an opening nearer half an inch. Across the Northeast, the rat inside a house is usually a Norway rat, though that is a regional tendency and no guarantee about your attic.

Habits matter too. Rats dig, so they show up along foundations, under sheds and beneath decks. Mice climb and squeeze, so they travel through wall cavities and cabinet backs. The technician confirms the species on site before quoting, since traps, mesh and cleanup all depend on it.

October is when the move-in starts.

Once nights approach freezing, outdoor food runs short and heated buildings begin to look like hotels. Garages, attics and crawl spaces on Long Island commonly see activity from October on, and it can last until the ground softens. A home that is closed up after Labor Day may go unobserved all winter, so the first pellet could turn up in spring.

  • Inspect the lower corners of the garage door and its rubber seal before the first hard frost.
  • Circle the foundation and look through every vent, pipe sleeve and cable entry for daylight.
  • Pour pet food, birdseed and grass mix into containers with tight lids.
  • Lift stacked logs onto a rack set a few feet off the wall.

Your first hour after noticing signs.

Three habits protect the household and preserve the evidence. First, don't touch droppings. Second, keep kids and animals away from that room. Third, let any trap you've already put down stay exactly where it is.

  • Put a quarter next to pellets and gnaw marks, then take a picture.
  • Note which room and which hour you noticed each sound.
  • Close off the room where sounds are loudest.
  • Skip poison bait and glue boards, which cause trouble of their own.

How the work unfolds.

A lone mouse in a pantry and a nest under a boat cover call for different tools, but both jobs follow the same four stages.

  1. 1

    Look around.

    The technician begins at the freshest evidence and then circles outward, hunting for pellets, burrows and the openings that animals really use.

  2. 2

    Set the traps.

    Devices go along the runs the evidence reveals. They are chosen for the species at hand and kept where kids and animals cannot get at them.

  3. 3

    Shut the gaps.

    Vents, cracks and chewed holes are covered using flashing, hardware cloth or sealant, all materials that rodent teeth can't beat.

  4. 4

    Come back.

    A second trip empties the traps, tests each repair and picks up whatever cleanup or restoration still remains.

Evidence that visitors have taken up residence.

Finding two or more together, in one corner, usually means current activity and not an old problem.

  • Droppings.

    Dark pellets beneath a sink, behind an appliance or sprinkled over attic insulation.

  • Chewing.

    Recent gnawing is pale and ragged, and it turns up on rafters, plastic plumbing, wire jackets and carton corners.

  • Sounds after dark.

    Scratching, light footsteps or a soft thump inside walls or overhead, usually once the house has settled.

  • Nesting scraps.

    Shredded paper, pulled insulation or cloth bits packed into a sheltered nook.

  • Greasy streaks.

    Dark, oily smears along baseboards, pipes and joists show a path that an animal walks nightly.

  • Smells.

    An ammonia sting inside a closed room, or a thick, sweetish rot wherever an animal has died.

Spot a couple of these? Phone us while the harm is still small.

Why surroundings change the plan.

Landmarks help us read a map, but they say nothing about what is inside your walls. A dead-end lane, a parcel that touches a marsh and a shop beside a loading dock each get a different opening move.

Machines count as buildings, too. A pickup, camper or trailer that has idled for weeks may hold a nest, and so can the shrink-wrap or canvas over a boat in the side yard. Lift the hood, check the cabin vents, and trust your nose. One chewed wire found early costs far less than a replaced harness.

Houses that sit shut for months.

Some South Shore homes are lived in only during the warm months, and others are occupied year-round. A shut, dim house suits rodents, and nobody is around to spot the first droppings. On your way out, use up the open food, tighten the gaps around the garage door, and ask a neighbor to look inside now and then. Returning in spring, check the attic hatch before you unpack.

Questions that come up before booking.

Mouse or rat: how can I be sure?

Look at the pellets. Mouse droppings are tiny and rice-shaped, and rat droppings are clearly bigger. Rats often work along foundations or overhead, while mice prefer kitchens, pantries and wall cavities.

Do waterfront houses get in through different openings?

The entrances are the familiar ones: dryer and bath vents, soffit panels, door sweeps, pipe sleeves and the edge of the roof. Salt air can rust weak metal covers, so a corroded vent screen deserves replacing.

Isn't trapping enough?

Traps take out the animals already inside. Sealing keeps new arrivals out, so we do both jobs in that order and then come back to check the result.

Is anything used that could hurt my kids or pets?

Tell us about every child and animal in the household when you call. Devices are either enclosed or kept well out of reach, and before leaving, the technician walks you through where each one sits.

How soon could someone arrive?

Someone answers the phone at all hours, and when the day's calendar has a gap, a same-day appointment goes to the first caller who asks.

What happens after the first visit?

On the second visit the technician checks the traps, pushes on every seal and handles any cleanup or repair, with dates you receive up front.
