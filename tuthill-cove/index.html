What we know about Tuthill Cove, and what we don't.

In practice that means you describe your property to us. The call takes a few minutes, and the street address you give tells us where to send the technician and which of our nearby coverage areas applies.

If "cove" means water close to your house.

A cove is a sheltered bend of shoreline. We cannot say how near any particular house is to the water, and some houses under this name may be well away from it. If yours does sit close to a bay edge, marsh grass or a tidal creek, a few general South Shore points apply.

  • Wrack, driftwood and tangled branches left by storms make ideal cover when they sit against a wall or deck.
  • Sandy ground is easy for a rat to dig, and burrows beside a foundation can shift after heavy rain.
  • Salt air may corrode plain steel vent screens, so a rusted screen deserves replacing with coated or stainless mesh.
  • Docks, bulkheads and boat gear stored close to the house can give animals a covered route to the door.

None of these is a flaw in a property. They are places a technician looks first, and they are quick for you to check before anyone arrives.

Year-round homes and seasonal ones.

Some homes on the South Shore are lived in only during the warm months, while others house families through every season. A house that stands dark from October to April gives rodents a long, undisturbed stretch, and the first sign may be a nest or a chewed cable discovered in spring.

If your place is seasonal, a few habits help. Clear cupboards of opened food before leaving, seal pet food and seed in tubs, test the garage door gasket and arrange a monthly peek inside by someone you trust. When you return, check the attic hatch, the kitchen cabinets and the boat or vehicle covers before you unpack.

Nor'easters, flooding and rodent movement.

Strong coastal storms can flood low ground and flush burrows, and they can leave debris pressed against buildings. We cannot predict what any storm will do and we make no claim that calls rise afterward. We can say that a walk around the property once the weather settles is worth the ten minutes.

  • Look for fresh holes, slumped soil and new runs in the grass.
  • Check vent covers, crawl space doors and garage door corners for damage.
  • Clear debris that has piled against siding or under decks.
  • Photograph anything odd and note the date, since the photos help the technician later.

Until a technician arrives.

Let pellets lie, keep kids and pets out of that room, and do not move traps already set. Avoid scattering bait or laying sticky boards, which bring dangers of their own. If cleaning cannot wait, the CDC box below shows the safe way to handle the waste.

When you call, mention the room, the hour of day and what you heard, saw or smelled. A brief, concrete account speeds the first visit more than a long tale.

How a visit works here.

The technician starts where the evidence is freshest, circles the exterior, notes openings and sets stations along real runs. Repairs come after early catches reveal the entrances still in use, and a second visit checks that they hold. The sequence is the same as anywhere else in the hamlet, adjusted for water, wind and the way your house is used.

Before you call, what public health agencies advise.

These federal pages are summarized in loose terms. For health questions, a doctor is the better source than a pest company.

  • If the tool in your hand is dry, put it down: brooms, vacuums, compressed air and dusters all qualify.
  • Create a cross-breeze, wait roughly half an hour, and only then step in without sniffing around.
  • Thin disposable gloves handle small messes, and heavier rubber pairs handle bigger ones and can be rinsed afterward.
  • A mix of one part bleach to nine parts water is the CDC's home recipe, and it should be made fresh each time.
  • Drop soiled towels and any dead animal into one plastic bag, using gloves or tongs, then tie it and place it in the household trash.
  • Mention the rodent contact to your doctor if illness appears within a few weeks.
