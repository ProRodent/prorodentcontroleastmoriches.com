The yard is where the job starts.

Most rodents that end up in a house lived outdoors first. They need food, water and cover, and they will use any lot that supplies them. The more of those a yard provides, the more animals nearby, and the more likely one of them tries your walls.

Yard work does not replace sealing the house, but it shrinks the pressure on every door and vent. It is the cheapest part of a rodent plan, and it often is the part that decides whether a repair job stays done.

Spotting a rodent route.

  • Burrow holes with smooth, packed edges, often along foundations, under sheds or beneath decks.
  • Narrow, flattened runs in grass leading from cover to a food source.
  • Grease marks on fence rails, sill plates and the base of walls.
  • Chewed seed bags, gnawed fruit and tooth marks on plastic bins.

Lots along the South Shore range from small fenced squares to sandy parcels with grass at the edges. Sand is easy to dig, so burrows can be extensive, and storm wash can hide old holes under new drifts. Walk the property slowly after a storm and look for fresh soil.

Water, wrack and storm debris.

Properties near marshes, creeks and the bay may see seaweed, branches and driftwood pile up after a storm. Heaps against a wall are ideal cover for a rat. Clear them promptly, and do not stack boat gear or tarps against siding.

Salt air can corrode metal sheds and fences over time, leaving gaps at the bottom. Check them each spring.

Traps, burrow work and sequence.

The technician sets stations on the runs first, waits for catches to slow, and then fills and caps burrows. Filling first only moves the animals. Once the yard is quiet, we close gaps around outbuildings and review the habits that kept the pressure high.

Pair this with the rat control page if you have confirmed burrows, or with sealing when the house itself needs work.

Seasonal homes and yard care.

Properties used only in summer can go months without a visitor to notice a burrow. Ask a neighbor to check the yard monthly in winter, and walk it yourself the day you arrive in spring.

Between the call and the visit.

  • Clear a route to the back of the lot and warn us about any dog that roams there.
  • Leave overgrown vines against the wall uncut until we have inspected them.
  • Have a torch ready for gloomy corners.
  • Capture burrows, pellets and bedding on camera before any raking.

Before you call, a protective checklist.

A slow pace means less grit drifting toward your face.

  • Pellets on a shelf look harmless, yet scuffing them frees fine grit, which is why dry methods are discouraged.
  • Fresh air does a share of the work, so let the space breathe with nobody in it.
  • Skin should not meet pellets, urine spots or shredded nest material, so wear gloves from the first minute.
  • Paper towels, not dry rags, pick up the wet waste, and you keep spraying until nothing looks powdery.
  • Ordinary garbage service takes the bag, and any reusable tool you used gets rinsed.
  • Mention the rodent contact to your doctor if illness appears within a few weeks.

Yard and outdoor questions.

May I shovel soil into the holes?

Wait until the animals are gone. Packing a burrow that is still occupied sends residents to a fresh spot, frequently nearer the house. Our crew traps first and fills afterward.

Do seed feeders bring rats around?

Fallen seed does. A feeder with a tray or a seed catcher, cleaned up daily, causes far fewer problems than one that rains seed onto the lawn.

Does tall grass matter?

Dense grass and brush along fences give animals cover between a burrow and a food source. Mowing and trimming shorten their covered route.

What about compost?

A closed bin with a solid floor is safer than an open pile. Avoid putting meat, bones or cooked food in any compost.

Do you handle sheds and decks?

Yes. Gaps under sheds, decks and porch steps are common bases for rodents, and the technician checks them on every outdoor visit.
